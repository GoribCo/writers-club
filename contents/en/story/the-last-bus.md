The timetable said 23:52. My phone said 23:51. The street said nothing at all, because in Cottbus the street goes to bed at nine.

I stood under the yellow light of the stop and practised my German. *Eine Fahrkarte, bitte.* *Zum Hauptbahnhof.* *Entschuldigung, ist das der letzte Bus?* I had been here six weeks and I could order bread, apologise, and ask about the last bus. That felt like enough to survive.

At 23:58 I began to compose the message to my mother. *Mum, I am fine, I will walk, it is only forty minutes, the cold is not so bad.* All lies except the forty minutes.

At 00:03 headlights turned the corner.

The driver was an old man with a moustache like a broom. He looked at me, at my thin jacket, at my hands that had stopped feeling like hands. Before I could say any of my sentences he said, in English:

"Ghana?"

"Yes. How did you know?"

He pointed at my scarf. Red, gold, green. My mother had knitted it in July, in Kumasi, at thirty-two degrees, and I had laughed at her.

"My wife's nurse is from Accra," he said. "Very good woman. Sit near the heater."

He did not take my money. I did not argue. I sat near the heater and watched the dark town slide by, and for the first time since September I did not feel like a visitor.

I sent my mother a different message: *The scarf works.*
